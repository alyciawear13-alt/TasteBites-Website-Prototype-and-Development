Safe food handling guide

For diners, servers and cooks. Plain-language basics, not medical advice.

What is cross-contact?

Cross-contact (often called cross-contamination) happens when an allergen gets into food that doesn't normally contain it. Cooking does not remove an allergen.

Common ways it happens:

  • Shared fryer oil (fries cooked with breaded shrimp)
  • The same cutting board, knife, grill or tongs
  • Flour in the air in a bakery or pasta kitchen
  • Gloves or hands that touched another dish
  • Pre-made sauces with hidden ingredients from suppliers

How it affects the customer

Even a tiny amount can cause a reaction. Depending on the person this can mean hives, stomach pain, trouble breathing, or anaphylaxis, which is a medical emergency. For people with celiac disease, gluten damages the small intestine even when there are no obvious symptoms.

Beyond the physical risk, many diners feel anxious or like a burden when eating out. Clear information up front takes that pressure off.

The server-to-kitchen handoff

Most mistakes happen when allergy information gets lost between the diner, the server and the cook. Good practice:

  1. Diner: say the allergy clearly, and how serious it is.
  2. Server: mark the ticket (many kitchens use a special color) and tell the chef in person.
  3. Kitchen: wash hands, change gloves, use clean or dedicated tools, check every ingredient label.
  4. Delivery: the allergy plate is carried out separately and confirmed with the diner.
